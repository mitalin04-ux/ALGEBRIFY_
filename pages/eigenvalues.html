Module 10

Diagonalization, Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors reveal the intrinsic invariant axes of linear operators where matrices act as pure scalar stretching. In this module, you will solve characteristic polynomials, apply the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem, analyze minimal polynomials, diagonalize matrices ($A = PDP^{-1}$), and explore Jordan Canonical Form.

Read Lesson Eigen-Solver Practice Quiz

Learning Objectives

  • State the fundamental eigenvalue equation $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}$ ($\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$).
  • Formulate and solve the Characteristic Equation $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.
  • Determine eigenspaces and compare algebraic vs geometric multiplicities.
  • Apply the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem ($p(A) = O$) to evaluate matrix powers and inverses.
  • Define minimal polynomials, derogatory matrices, matrix diagonalization ($A = PDP^{-1}$), and Jordan Canonical Form.
Section 10.1

Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors and the Characteristic Equation

Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ square matrix. A scalar $\lambda$ is called an eigenvalue of $A$ if there exists a non-zero vector $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ such that:

Fundamental Eigenvalue Equation:
\[ A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v} \iff (A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \]

For a non-zero eigenvector $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ to exist, the matrix $(A - \lambda I)$ must be singular, meaning its determinant must be zero:

\[ p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 \quad (\text{The Characteristic Equation}) \]
Worked Example

Finding Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a $2 \times 2$ Matrix

Find the eigenvalues and corresponding eigenvectors of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step 1: Set up the Characteristic Equation:

\[ \det(A - \lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 4 - \lambda & 1 \\ 2 & 3 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} = (4 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - (1)(2) = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0 \]

Step 2: Factor and Solve for $\lambda$:

\[ (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 5, \quad \lambda_2 = 2 \]

Step 3: Eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 5$:

\[ (A - 5I)\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies -v_1 + v_2 = 0 \implies \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Step 4: Eigenvector for $\lambda_2 = 2$:

\[ (A - 2I)\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies 2v_1 + v_2 = 0 \implies \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \]
Section 10.2

The Cayley-Hamilton Theorem & Minimal Polynomial

The Cayley-Hamilton Theorem:

Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial. That is, if $p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = (-1)^n \lambda^n + c_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + c_0$, then:

\[ p(A) = (-1)^n A^n + c_{n-1} A^{n-1} + \dots + c_0 I = O \]
Application

Computing $A^{-1}$ using Cayley-Hamilton

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, the characteristic equation was $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0$.

By Cayley-Hamilton:

\[ A^2 - 7A + 10I = O \implies 10I = 7A - A^2 \]

Multiplying both sides by $A^{-1}$:

\[ 10 A^{-1} = 7I - A \implies A^{-1} = \frac{1}{10}(7I - A) = \frac{1}{10}\left( \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 0 \\ 0 & 7 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \right) = \begin{bmatrix} 0.3 & -0.1 \\ -0.2 & 0.4 \end{bmatrix} \]

Minimal Polynomial ($m(\lambda)$)

The unique monic polynomial of least degree such that $m(A) = O$. It always divides the characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda)$.

Derogatory Matrix

A matrix is called derogatory if the degree of its minimal polynomial is strictly less than its order ($\deg(m) < n$). Otherwise, it is non-derogatory.

Section 10.3

Matrix Diagonalization ($A = PDP^{-1}$) & Jordan Canonical Form

Diagonalization Theorem

An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if it has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors:

\[ A = P D P^{-1} \]

Where $P = [\mathbf{v}_1 \dots \mathbf{v}_n]$ and $D = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$.

Rapid Matrix Powers

Diagonalization computes high powers effortlessly:

\[ A^k = P D^k P^{-1} = P \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1^k & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2^k \end{bmatrix} P^{-1} \]

Jordan Canonical Form

When a matrix lacks $n$ independent eigenvectors (defective), it can be converted to a block-diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks:

\[ J_k(\lambda) = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \lambda \end{bmatrix} \]

Key Points

  • $\sum \lambda_i = \text{tr}(A)$ (sum of eigenvalues = trace).
  • $\prod \lambda_i = \det(A)$ (product of eigenvalues = determinant).
  • Cayley-Hamilton: $p(A) = O$.
  • $A$ is diagonalizable $\iff$ Geometric Multiplicity = Algebraic Multiplicity for all eigenvalues.

Common Mistakes

  • An eigenvector can NEVER be the zero vector ($\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ by definition). An eigenvalue, however, CAN be zero ($\lambda = 0$).
  • Do not assume all matrices are diagonalizable; if a matrix has repeated eigenvalues with insufficient independent eigenvectors, Jordan form is required.
