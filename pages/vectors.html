Module 04

Vectors

A vector is a mathematical quantity possessing both magnitude and direction. In this module, you will study vectors both geometrically as directed arrows and algebraically as ordered coordinate tuples, master vector addition and scalar multiplication, and learn dot and 3D cross products.

Read Lesson Vector Calculator Practice Quiz

Learning Objectives

  • Understand geometric and algebraic representations of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
  • Classify standard vector types: zero, unit, position, and collinear vectors.
  • Compute vector addition, scalar multiplication, magnitudes, and unit vectors.
  • Apply the dot product to compute angles, project vectors, and test orthogonality.
  • Compute 3D cross products, normal vectors, and parallelogram areas.
Section 4.1

Geometric & Algebraic Representations

Vectors can be viewed from two complementary perspectives: geometric and algebraic.

1. Geometric Representation

A directed line segment $\vec{AB}$ having an initial point $A$ and a terminal point $B$.

  • Magnitude: Length of the segment, denoted by $\|\mathbf{v}\|$ or $|\mathbf{v}|$.
  • Direction: Indicated by the arrowhead pointing from $A$ to $B$.

2. Algebraic Representation

An ordered column of $n$ real numbers representing coordinates in Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$:

\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n \]

Vector Magnitude & Unit Vectors

The magnitude (Euclidean norm) of vector $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{bmatrix}$ is:

\[ \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + v_3^2} \]

A unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ has magnitude $1$ and points in the same direction: $\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}$.

Section 4.2

Types of Vectors & Elementary Operations

1. Zero Vector ($\mathbf{0}$)

A vector with magnitude $0$ and undefined/arbitrary direction: $\mathbf{0} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

2. Position Vector

A vector representing the coordinates of a point $P(x, y, z)$ relative to the origin $O(0, 0, 0)$: $\vec{OP} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}$.

3. Collinear Vectors

Vectors that lie along the same or parallel lines. Algebraically, $\mathbf{u} = k\mathbf{v}$ for some scalar $k \neq 0$.

4. Linear Combination

Combining addition and scalar multiplication: $\mathbf{w} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k$ where $c_i \in \mathbb{R}$.

Section 4.3

The Dot Product (Scalar Product)

Definition of Dot Product ($\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$):

For vectors $\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \\ \dots \\ u_n \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \dots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$:

\[ \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + \dots + u_n v_n = \|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\| \cos\theta \]

Where $\theta$ ($0 \le \theta \le \pi$) is the angle between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.

Worked Example

Angle Between Two Vectors

Find the angle between $\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Dot product: $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = (1)(2) + (2)(2) + (2)(-1) = 2 + 4 - 2 = \mathbf{4}$.
  2. Magnitudes: $\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{9} = \mathbf{3}$, and $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{2^2 + 2^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{9} = \mathbf{3}$.
  3. Angle Formula: $\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|} = \frac{4}{3 \times 3} = \frac{4}{9} \approx 0.444$.
  4. $\theta = \arccos\left(\frac{4}{9}\right) \approx 63.6^\circ$.

Important Properties of the Dot Product:

  • Commutative: $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}$.
  • Distributive: $\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w}$.
  • Orthogonality Test: Two non-zero vectors are perpendicular ($\theta = 90^\circ$) if and only if $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.
  • Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality: $|\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}| \le \|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\|$.
Section 4.4

The 3D Cross Product (Vector Product)

Definition of Cross Product ($\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$):

The cross product is defined strictly in 3-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$. It produces a vector that is perpendicular to both $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$:

\[ \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2 \\ u_3 v_1 - u_1 v_3 \\ u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1 \end{bmatrix} \]
Worked Example

Computing a Cross Product & Parallelogram Area

Compute $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ for $\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Calculation:

\[ \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} (2)(6) - (3)(5) \\ (3)(4) - (1)(6) \\ (1)(5) - (2)(4) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 - 15 \\ 12 - 6 \\ 5 - 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 6 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Area of Parallelogram: $\|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 6^2 + (-3)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 36 + 9} = \sqrt{54} \approx 7.35$ sq units.

Important Properties of the Cross Product:

  • Anti-Commutative: $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = -(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{u})$.
  • $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$ for any vector $\mathbf{u}$.
  • Two non-zero vectors are parallel ($\theta = 0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$) if and only if $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.
  • $\|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\| = \|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\| \sin\theta$.

Key Points

  • The dot product $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}$ yields a scalar.
  • The cross product $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}$ yields a vector (in $\mathbb{R}^3$).
  • $\mathbf{u} \perp \mathbf{v} \iff \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.
  • $\mathbf{u} \parallel \mathbf{v} \iff \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.
  • $\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v} = -(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{u})$ (cross product is anti-commutative).

Common Mistakes

  • Do not attempt cross products in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^4$; standard cross product is defined only in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  • Do not forget the negative sign when computing the $y$-component of a cross product: $-(u_1 v_3 - u_3 v_1) = u_3 v_1 - u_1 v_3$.
